ROTTNEST CHANNEL SWIM

The world's largest open water swimming event, drawing over 2,500 participants each February for the 19.7km crossing from Cottesloe Beach to Thomson Bay on Rottnest Island. Open to solos, duos, and teams of four.

What Makes It Challenging

Primary Hazards

  • The channel is open ocean — swells of 1–3m from the southwest are common in February, creating relentless side chop that increases energy expenditure significantly.
  • Blue-ringed octopus and stinging jellyfish (particularly Chrysaora species) are present in these waters, though encounters during the event are rare.
  • Current variability: the Leeuwin Current can push swimmers off course and create fatigue-inducing drift that adds distance if navigation is poor.
  • Heat and sun exposure over a multi-hour swim in February's summer sun can cause dehydration — liquid nutrition management is critical.

Conditions Overview

  • Swell direction is typically from the southwest, hitting swimmers on the right side for most of the crossing heading westward.
  • Wind chop overlays the swell in the afternoon; most elite swimmers aim to finish before conditions deteriorate after midday.
  • Water clarity is exceptional — visibility often exceeds 10m — which can be psychologically challenging over deep, open ocean.
  • The final kilometres approaching Thomson Bay often see a current pushing swimmers north; pilots guide boat-swimmer pairs to compensate.
About The Crossing

The Rottnest Channel Swim began in 1990 with a handful of swimmers and has grown into one of the iconic events on the global open water calendar. The channel between the Western Australian mainland and Rottnest Island — known to the Noongar people as Wadjemup — covers 19.7km of exposed Indian Ocean. The crossing departs from Cottesloe Beach, one of Perth's most beloved swimming beaches, and finishes at Thomson Bay on the island's eastern shore.

What makes the Rottnest Swim unique in world open water swimming is its scale: over 2,500 swimmers enter across solo, duo, and team-of-four categories, supported by hundreds of escort vessels and thousands of volunteers. The event is so popular that entries typically sell out within minutes of opening, often months in advance. The presence of so many swimmers on the water simultaneously creates a festive, community atmosphere very different from solitary marathon crossings.

Solo swimming is the most demanding category and draws an international field of elite marathon swimmers each year. The current solo record is held by Tommy Mayne (2019) at 3 hours 56 minutes. The duo category — where two swimmers alternate in 90-minute legs — is the most popular entry point for recreational swimmers tackling the channel for the first time.

Every swimmer must have a registered escort vessel and a paddler or kayaker alongside them in the water. Pilots are experienced local operators who know the current patterns of the channel intimately and guide swimmers on the most efficient line to Thomson Bay.

Preparation Guide

Training Benchmarks (Solo)

  • Longest single open water swim of at least 12–15km before race day.
  • Consistent weekly volume of 25–35km in the 8 weeks prior to the event.
  • Swell training: practise in ocean swells to adapt your stroke rhythm and breathing to a moving waterline.
  • Feed practice: can you eat and drink from a kayak or boat while treading water or swimming slowly? Practise this in open water.
  • For duo and team entries, coordination and transition drills between swimmers in open water are important.

Unlike many marathon crossings, the Rottnest Swim takes place in relatively warm water (18–22°C), which removes the cold water acclimatisation demand that dominates preparation for northern hemisphere crossings. The primary training focus for solos is distance endurance and swell tolerance. If you're training in protected pool or harbour water, build in at least one long ocean swim per week through your preparation block to adapt to the physical and psychological demands of open water movement.

Nutrition planning is as important as physical preparation for solos. A 5–7 hour swim in summer heat requires disciplined hydration and carbohydrate intake. Most experienced Rottnest soloists take a liquid feed (electrolyte drink with maltodextrin) every 20–30 minutes. Brief the paddler supporting you in water on your feeding schedule and what to watch for — signs of heat stress, salt intake, and stroke count degradation are the key indicators your paddler should monitor.

Frequently Asked Questions
Can I wear a wetsuit?
Yes. Unlike English Channel-style crossings, wetsuits are permitted in all categories of the Rottnest Channel Swim. However, solo category results are split into wetsuit and non-wetsuit divisions, and the overall solo winners are typically non-wetsuit swimmers. Many recreational participants choose wetsuits for buoyancy and comfort over the longer distance.
How do entries work — are they hard to get?
Extremely. Entries open once per year (typically July–August for the following February event) and sell out within hours, sometimes minutes. Solo entries are particularly limited. Register for notifications on the official Rottnest Channel Swim website and be ready to enter the moment the ballot or direct sale opens. Many swimmers enter the ballot multiple years in a row.
What is a pilot and do I need one?
A registered pilot is a licensed boat operator who escorts your swimmer or team across the channel. Every entry must have a registered pilot vessel — this is mandatory and cannot be substituted by a friend with a tinnie. Pilots are booked through the official pilot registration system and must meet event safety requirements. Most swimmers book pilots as soon as their entry is confirmed.
Is there a minimum qualifier required?
Solo swimmers must demonstrate a qualifying swim: typically a sanctioned open water swim of at least 10km completed within a specified timeframe. Duo and team-of-four entrants have lower qualifying requirements. Full qualifying standards are published on the official event website and updated annually.
What happens if weather is too rough on race day?
The event has a dedicated safety and weather committee. If conditions are deemed unsafe (typically swell height exceeding safe thresholds), the event is postponed to the following day or, in rare cases, cancelled. This has occurred a handful of times in the event's history. Swimmers should plan accommodation and travel with the possibility of a one-day postponement in mind.
