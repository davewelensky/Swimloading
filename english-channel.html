Water Temp by Month — Dover Strait
Swim season runs late June to early October. September and October are the warmest months due to thermal lag; the trade-off is shorter days and deteriorating weather. Qualifying swims (6h in ≤16°C) are typically done in May–June.
How Pilots Work
A registered pilot is mandatory for a CSA- or CS&PF-ratified crossing — they navigate, judge conditions, and witness the swim. Pilots book swimmers in tide windows (a 5–7 day slot, ranked 1st through ~7th position). Position 1 swims first when weather lifts; later positions wait their turn. Bookings are typically taken 2 years in advance with a deposit of £700–£1,200.

Tide Window Allocation
1st position — swims first when weather lifts; gets the best window
2nd–3rd — usually swim within 2–4 days of the leader
4th–7th — may swim later in tide; risk window closing
Standby — picks up if higher position cancels
Practical reality: Most swimmers in positions 4+ build up to a year of extra training and travel costs because a single tide window may not deliver swimmable weather for everyone in line. Booking earlier secures better positions in popular weeks.

Frequently Asked Questions
20 questions about swimming the English Channel — answered for first-time attempters, qualifying swimmers, and the merely curious.
How cold is the English Channel?

The Channel ranges from 7–9°C in winter to 17–18°C at its warmest in September. The swim season (June–October) sees water temperatures of 14–18°C. Qualifying swims require water ≤16°C.

How long does it take to swim the English Channel?

Average crossing time is 12–16 hours. The world record is 6h 45m 25s (Andreas Waschburger, September 2023). The slowest ratified crossings have taken over 27 hours.

How much does it cost to swim the English Channel?

A solo attempt costs £4,500–£8,500 all-in. Pilot fees alone are £3,000–£4,500. Add governing body registration (~£155–£200), observer fees (~£200–£300), medical certificate, training travel, and 5–7 nights of Dover accommodation during the weather wait.

What is the success rate?

Roughly 50% of attempts succeed. Common reasons for not finishing include hypothermia, severe seasickness, shoulder failure, and missing the tide window.

When can you swim the English Channel?

The official CSA season is April 1 to October 31, but the practical swim window is late June to early October. Peak booking months are July–September. September has the warmest water (17–18°C) but more unpredictable weather.

Do you wear a wetsuit?

For a CSA or CS&PF ratified crossing, no. Only a standard swimming costume, one cap, goggles and ear plugs are permitted. Wetsuit crossings exist but are recorded separately and are not eligible for the official record.

What is the qualifying swim?

You must complete a 6-hour continuous swim in water ≤16°C, witnessed by an approved observer, in the calendar year of your Channel attempt. Many swimmers qualify during Dover Harbour training sessions in May–June.

How far is the English Channel swim?

The straight-line distance from Shakespeare Beach (Dover) to Cap Gris-Nez (France) is approximately 33 km (21 miles). Due to tidal sweep, swimmers typically cover 40–50 km in the water.

How many people have swum the English Channel?

Over 2,500 ratified solo crossings have been recorded across the CSA and CS&PF since Matthew Webb's first in 1875. Roughly 1,900+ unique individuals — many swimmers cross multiple times.

Who holds the English Channel world record?

Andreas Waschburger (Germany) holds the men's solo record at 6h 45m 25s, set September 2023. Yvetta Hlavacova (Czech Republic) holds the women's record at 7h 25m from 2006.

What is the youngest age to swim the English Channel?

The youngest ever was Tom Gregory at 11 in 1988. Minimum ages have since been set at 16 (CSA) and 17 (CS&PF). The oldest successful swimmer was Otto Thaning at 73 in 2014.

How do English Channel tides work?

Semi-diurnal — two highs and two lows per ~24h. Spring tides (HW Dover >6.1m) bring stronger currents and greater tidal sweep. Neap tides (HW <6.1m) are slower and more predictable. The cycle repeats every ~14 days. Most swimmers book neap windows.

How dangerous are the shipping lanes?

The Channel is the world's busiest shipping lane (~600 tankers and ~200 ferries daily) but a registered pilot navigates around all traffic. Swimmers cross perpendicular to the CNIS lanes; the pilot is responsible for separation.

Are there jellyfish?

Yes. Compass jellyfish appear from July. Lion's mane jellyfish peak in late August and September. Most swimmers experience some stings; few are forced to abandon by jellyfish alone.

What do Channel swimmers eat during the crossing?

Most feed every 30–45 minutes from the pilot boat — typically a carbohydrate drink (Maxim, Carbo Pro at ~10%) plus solids like jelly babies, bananas and ginger. Feeds are 30–60 seconds standing-down only — no contact with the boat or supporters.

What weather conditions are needed?

Pilots typically call no-swim at Beaufort Force 4 sustained (≥11kt). Ideal conditions: wind ≤10kt, waves ≤1.2m, water temp ≥16°C, minimal swell. Decisions are made the evening before each potential swim day.

What happens if you don't finish?

The pilot will lift you out when you ask, when conditions deteriorate, or when the observer judges the swim unsafe. There's no ratification, but most pilots allow another attempt in a future tide window without a full re-deposit.

How do you train for the English Channel?

Most swimmers train for 18–24 months, building from 5 km/week to 50+ km/week. Cold-water acclimatisation is critical: 6–12 months of swimming in cold open water in a costume only. Most British swimmers train at Dover Harbour on weekends from May.

Is the English Channel swim harder than an Ironman?

Most athletes who have done both say the Channel is significantly harder. Channel swims last 12–16+ hours non-stop in 14–18°C water with no rest, vs ~10–12 hours of Ironman racing. Hypothermia risk and the lack of mid-event recovery make the Channel uniquely brutal.

Who was the first person to swim the English Channel?

Captain Matthew Webb completed the first ratified crossing on 24–25 August 1875 in 21h 45m. Gertrude Ederle became the first woman in 1926 — her time of 14h 39m was faster than all five previous male crossings.
