MANHATTAN ISLAND MARATHON SWIM

A ~48km circumnavigation of Manhattan Island through the Hudson, Harlem, and East Rivers, completing the Triple Crown of Open Water Swimming. Tidal timing is everything — the swim only works if you ride the currents correctly.

~48 km 16–24°C Very Hard Jun – Sep
Key Facts
Distance
~48 km
The circumnavigation covers the full perimeter of Manhattan Island — Hudson River south, Battery Park turn, East River north, Harlem River west, and back to start.

Temperatures peak in July–August. The Hudson and East Rivers can differ by 2–3°C. Early season (June) can be as low as 16°C.

Jun – Sep

Record: 5h 45m (Shelley Taylor-Smith, 1995). Most competitors finish in 7–10 hours. The tidal window closes around 9–10 hours.

MIMS
Manhattan Island Marathon Swim organisation. Sanctioned swims require MIMS approval, an official observer, and coordination with the US Coast Guard and NYC port authorities.
Difficulty
Very Hard
Part of the Triple Crown. The raw distance is manageable for experienced marathon swimmers, but tidal and current management makes it a genuine navigation and pacing challenge.
What Makes It Challenging

Primary Hazards

  • Tidal window is rigid: the swim must start at a precise time to ride the flood tide up the East River and ebb down the Hudson. Miss the window and you swim against current — virtually impossible to finish.
  • Heavy commercial vessel traffic — ferries, barges, and tugs — operates continuously. Your escort boat must actively shield you from wash and maintain communication with port control.
  • Water quality varies significantly by section — the Harlem River historically has the poorest water quality of the three waterways, with elevated bacterial counts after rain.
  • Debris in the water: logs, plastic, and urban detritus are common, particularly after rain events. Eye and mouth protection matters.

Conditions Overview

  • Currents run up to 4–5 knots in the East River narrows — with the tide this is an enormous assist; against it, progress is nearly impossible.
  • The Hell Gate section (junction of East River, Harlem River, and Long Island Sound) produces complex, turbulent water with boils and eddies that can spin swimmers off course.
  • Wind funnelling between Manhattan's skyscrapers can create localised chop in the Hudson despite benign offshore conditions.
  • Water temperatures in the Hudson River can change rapidly with tidal exchange — be prepared for cold patches without warning.
About The Crossing

The Manhattan Island Marathon Swim has been conducted since 1982, when Byron Bright became the first to complete the circumnavigation in an officially sanctioned event. The swim follows the perimeter of Manhattan Island: starting at the Battery (the island's southern tip), heading north up the Hudson River along the west side, turning east through the Spuyten Duyvil Creek into the Harlem River, continuing east and south through the East River, navigating the infamous Hell Gate, and finishing back at the Battery.

The entire challenge is built around tidal timing. The East River is not actually a river — it is a tidal strait connecting Upper New York Bay to Long Island Sound. The currents in the East River run north on the flood tide and south on the ebb. A swimmer must ride the flood north, reach Hell Gate before the tide turns, and then ride the ebb back south. Miss any of these transitions and the swim becomes physically impossible without extraordinary effort. MIMS calculates the precise start time for each attempt months in advance based on tidal predictions.

The swim is the third leg of the Triple Crown of Open Water Swimming, alongside the English Channel and the Catalina Channel. Completing all three earns a swimmer one of open water's most prestigious recognitions. As of 2024, fewer than 250 individuals have achieved the Triple Crown, reflecting the logistics, expense, and training required to complete three major crossings across different oceans.

The water quality of New York's rivers has improved dramatically since the 1970s but remains variable. The Clean Water Act's impact on the Hudson is remarkable — the river now supports fish and wildlife that had been absent for generations. However, combined sewer overflow events after heavy rain still degrade water quality temporarily, and MIMS monitors conditions closely before each sanctioned swim.

Preparation Guide

Training Benchmarks

  • Longest single open water swim of at least 30km — the sheer distance of MIMS requires proven ultra-distance capacity.
  • Weekly training volume of 50–60km in the 3 months prior to your attempt.
  • River and current swimming experience — practise in tidal or current-affected water to understand drift management and pacing adjustments.
  • Night swimming experience — many MIMS starts are in early morning darkness for tidal window reasons.
  • Feed efficiency: eating and drinking must not break your stroke rhythm. Practise continuous swimming with feed stops under 15 seconds.

The Manhattan Island circumnavigation is unusual among marathon swims in that speed matters more than in channel crossings — not because of distance, but because of the tidal window. Swimmers who fall behind pace risk missing the current transition at Hell Gate, which effectively ends the attempt. Your preparation should include tempo sets and race-pace training at realistic speeds, not just slow ultra-distance endurance work. Know your pace per kilometre in open water with high confidence.

Logistics preparation is as demanding as physical preparation. MIMS requires coordination with the US Coast Guard, New York City Department of Parks and Recreation, and port authorities. You need a MIMS-approved observer, a suitable escort vessel, and a pilot familiar with New York Harbor tidal patterns. Most swimmers apply to MIMS a year in advance for an official event slot, rather than attempting an independent sanctioned swim. The official annual MIMS event in June handles much of the permitting complexity on behalf of entrants.

Frequently Asked Questions
Why does the swim go counterclockwise around Manhattan?
The counterclockwise direction (down the Hudson, up the East River) is dictated by tidal timing. Swimmers ride the ebb tide south down the Hudson to the Battery, then the flood tide north up the East River and through Hell Gate. The tides in the two waterways operate on slightly offset schedules, making this the only direction that allows a swimmer to have current assistance for the majority of the course.
Is the water safe to swim in?
Generally yes, under normal conditions. MIMS monitors water quality and will postpone or cancel an attempt if bacterial levels are unsafe — typically after heavy rainfall that triggers sewer overflow. Swimmers are advised to keep their mouths closed when possible in the Harlem River section and to shower immediately after finishing. The rivers are dramatically cleaner than they were 40 years ago.
Are wetsuits permitted?
For a Triple Crown-ratified MIMS completion, English Channel rules apply: no wetsuit. The official MIMS event has historically been a non-wetsuit event. Independent sanctioned attempts seeking Triple Crown ratification must also be wetsuit-free. Check current MIMS rules as policies have evolved over time.
How do I enter the official MIMS event?
Apply through the MIMS website (swimaroundny.com). The annual event has limited entry and requires a demonstrated marathon swim history. Applications open in the preceding autumn. You can also apply for an independently sanctioned attempt outside the annual event, which requires more direct permitting work but offers more flexibility on date.
What happens at Hell Gate?
Hell Gate is the name for the turbulent confluence of the East River, Harlem River, and Long Island Sound near Astoria, Queens. The complex geometry of the waterways creates powerful eddies, standing waves, and boils when the tide is running strongly. Swimmers typically experience the most disorienting and physically demanding water of the entire circumnavigation in this section — usually around hours 4–6 of the swim.
