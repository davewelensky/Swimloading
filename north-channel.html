Oceans Seven · Ireland / Scotland

North Channel

Donaghadee, Northern Ireland to Portpatrick, Scotland. The coldest and most feared crossing in the Oceans Seven — and the hardest open water swim on earth.

~35 km 10–14°C Extreme Jul – Sep
Key Facts
Distance
~35 km
Straight-line distance. Tidal drift means swimmers cover 38–45 km in the water.
Water Temperature
10–14°C
Peak season (Aug–Sep) averages 12–14°C. Water below 10°C has been recorded in July.
Swim Season
Jul – Sep
Most successful crossings happen in late July and August. September sees more settled weather but cooling water.
Average Crossing Time
10–14 hrs
Record: 9 hours 32 minutes (Donaghadee to Portpatrick). Many crossings take 12+ hours.
Governing Body
ILDSA
The Irish Long Distance Swimming Association (ILDSA) sanctions and ratifies crossings. Piloting and support operations such as Infinity Channel Swimming run the escort boats.
Success Rate
~40%
Lower than the English Channel. Cold and jellyfish are the primary causes of failed attempts.
What Makes It So Hard

Primary Hazards

  • Water temperature as low as 10–12°C — sustained hypothermia risk through any attempt
  • Lion's mane jellyfish (Cyanea capillata) — the largest jellyfish in the world, peak season July–August
  • Powerful tidal streams that can push swimmers miles off course if mistimed
  • Shipping traffic in the North Channel maritime corridor

Conditions Overview

  • Neap tides are preferred — spring tides create current that makes the crossing nearly impossible
  • Wind windows are narrow; the Irish Sea is notorious for rapid weather changes
  • Sea state can deteriorate quickly — swells of 1–2m are common during attempts
  • Thick jellyfish blooms in some years mean contact is unavoidable at night
About The Crossing

The North Channel separates Northern Ireland from southwest Scotland. The shortest viable route runs from Donaghadee Harbour on the Ards Peninsula to Portpatrick on the Rhins of Galloway — roughly 35 km as the crow flies, considerably more through the water.

The first ratified crossing was completed by Tom Blower in 1947, after earlier attempts dating back to the late 19th century. For decades it remained one of the rarest accomplishments in open water swimming. It was designated one of the Oceans Seven by Steven Munatones in 2008, alongside the English Channel, Catalina, Cook Strait, Molokai Channel, Tsugaru Strait and Strait of Gibraltar.

The jellyfish problem is unlike anywhere else in the Oceans Seven. Lion's mane tentacles can trail 3–4 metres and deliver intense stings. Swimmers have reported full-body welts requiring post-swim hospitalisation. Many North Channel veterans describe the jellyfish as psychologically as hard as the cold.

Cold water acclimatisation is non-negotiable. Most successful swimmers spend 12–18 months swimming through Irish or Scottish winters, building tolerance below 10°C. No amount of English Channel preparation fully substitutes for North Channel cold.

Preparation Guide

Training Benchmarks

  • Peak weekly volume: 50–65 km/week in the 12 weeks before your attempt
  • Longest training swim: 8–10 hours continuous (not just distance — time matters)
  • Cold acclimatisation: regular swims at or below 12°C for 6+ months
  • Night swimming: at least 2–3 night ocean sessions in the months before (jellyfish are worse at night)
  • Feeding practice: comfortable taking feeds at 45-minute intervals without stopping

Most North Channel swimmers train in Ireland or the west coast of Scotland from October through the winter to build cold tolerance in conditions that replicate what they'll face on crossing day. Dover Harbour is useful for English Channel prep; the North Channel requires Irish Sea cold.

An ILDSA-ratified attempt typically requires completing a qualifying swim of at least 8 hours in cold water. Many pilots require evidence of cold water tolerance before they will accept a booking. Budget 2–3 years of preparation for most swimmers attempting this crossing.

Frequently Asked Questions
Why is the North Channel harder than the English Channel?
The English Channel is 2–4°C warmer and has virtually no lion's mane jellyfish. The North Channel's combination of cold water, powerful tides and jellyfish makes it significantly harder — most open water experts rate it the hardest of the Oceans Seven.
How do you deal with lion's mane jellyfish?
You don't avoid them — you endure them. Some swimmers use a thin layer of lanolin for minor protection. Most accept stings as part of the crossing. Antihistamines are carried on escort boats but rarely eliminate the pain. Mental preparation for jellyfish contact is considered as important as physical training.
How cold does the water get?
The warmest recorded crossing-day temperatures are around 14°C in late August. July attempts often see 10–12°C. In cold years, even September water can be 11°C. The crossing requires sustained swimming through temperatures that would cause hypothermia within 1–2 hours for an untrained swimmer.
What is the Oceans Seven?
The Oceans Seven is marathon swimming's equivalent of the Seven Summits — seven iconic channel crossings worldwide: English Channel, North Channel, Catalina Channel, Cook Strait, Molokai Channel, Tsugaru Strait and Strait of Gibraltar. The North Channel is widely regarded as the hardest of the seven.
How do I book a North Channel attempt?
Ratification is handled by the Irish Long Distance Swimming Association (ILDSA). Book an escort boat through a piloting and support operation such as Infinity Channel Swimming — pilots operate out of Donaghadee. Bookings are taken 12–18 months in advance for peak season windows. You will need evidence of a qualifying swim and cold-water experience before a pilot will accept your booking.
