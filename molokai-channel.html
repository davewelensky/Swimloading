MOLOKAI CHANNEL

Known in Hawaiian as Ka'iwi Channel — the Channel of Bones — this 42km crossing from Molokai to Oahu is one of the most physically demanding of the Oceans Seven. Warm water conceals deep Pacific swells, box jellyfish blooms, and one of the world's highest concentrations of tiger sharks.

Key Facts
Distance
~42 km
From Laau Point, Molokai to Sandy Beach, Oahu. Currents frequently push swimmers well off a straight line — actual distance swum is often 45–50km.
Water Temperature

The warmest of the Oceans Seven crossings. While comfortable for swimming, warm water supports year-round jellyfish presence and reduces any cold-shock risk management tool.
Swim Season
Apr – Sep
Summer months offer the best swell windows. The channel never has flat conditions — summer swells are smaller (1–2m) than winter trade-wind swells (3–5m).

14–18 hrs
Record: 12h 16m (Penny Palfrey, 2009). Most swimmers require 15–20 hours. The distance and swell exposure make it one of the longest Oceans Seven crossings in elapsed time.
Wildlife

Tiger sharks and box jellyfish (Alatina alata) are the two primary wildlife hazards. Box jellyfish bloom on a monthly lunar cycle — planning around this is essential.
Difficulty
Extreme
One of the Oceans Seven. The combination of distance, relentless Pacific swell, jellyfish risk, and psychological demands of 15+ hours in deep open ocean make this a supreme challenge.
What Makes It Challenging

Primary Hazards

  • Box jellyfish (Alatina alata) bloom predictably 9–10 nights after each full moon as they migrate from deep water to shallow reef areas — crossing dates must account for this cycle.
  • Tiger sharks are resident in Hawaiian waters year-round and are considered responsible for the majority of shark incidents in Hawaii. The channel's deep water is prime hunting territory.
  • Pacific trade wind swells of 1.5–3m hit swimmers from the northeast for the majority of the crossing, creating relentless energy expenditure and stroke disruption over 15+ hours.
  • The channel reaches depths of over 800m — the psychological reality of swimming over genuinely deep, dark open ocean for a full day is a significant mental challenge.

Conditions Overview

  • The Ka'iwi Channel is consistently rough — even in summer, the northeast trade winds generate a reliable 1–2m swell that rarely abates.
  • Strong surface currents can push swimmers significantly south of the target finish at Sandy Beach, adding kilometres to the actual distance covered.
  • Swims typically start at night from Molokai to take advantage of calmer pre-dawn conditions and arrive at Oahu in daylight.
  • Seasickness is a significant issue for support crew on escort boats in this channel — experienced ocean crews are essential.
About The Crossing

The Ka'iwi Channel — translated from Hawaiian as "Channel of Bones," named for the many sailors and fishermen who perished in its waters — is the stretch of Pacific Ocean between the islands of Molokai and Oahu. At approximately 42km, it is one of the longer Oceans Seven crossings, and the powerful trade wind swells that characterise Hawaiian waters mean swimmers face sustained rough-water conditions from start to finish. The channel has no shelter, no landmarks to aim at for most of the crossing, and depths that exceed 800 metres.

The first swim of the Ka'iwi Channel is attributed to Keo Nakama in 1961. The crossing gained international attention when Lynne Cox swam it in 1978 in 11 hours 32 minutes, a record that stood for many years. Today, the Molokai Channel is one of the most sought-after Oceans Seven crossings for international marathon swimmers, both because of its genuine difficulty and because Hawaii is a compelling destination for crew and support teams.

The box jellyfish planning constraint is unique among major crossings. Alatina alata, the box jellyfish species found in Hawaiian waters, migrates from deep water to reef areas in predictable blooms occurring roughly 9–10 days after each full moon. During these blooms, which typically last 2–3 nights, jellyfish numbers in the channel can be extraordinary — stings during a bloom can incapacitate a swimmer within minutes. All serious Ka'iwi Channel attempts are planned around the lunar calendar to avoid these windows, significantly constraining the scheduling of any given attempt.

Tiger sharks in Hawaiian waters are a genuine risk. Hawaii has the highest rate of unprovoked tiger shark incidents of any US state, and the deep, prey-rich waters of the Ka'iwi Channel are known shark habitat. Support boats maintain constant watch, and most experienced Hawaiian channel swimmers accept the risk as part of the challenge. Shark deterrent devices (electronic or magnetic) are sometimes used but are not universally effective against tiger sharks.

Preparation Guide

Training Benchmarks

  • Longest single open water swim of at least 30km — the Ka'iwi Channel demands proven ultra-distance capacity in rough water.
  • Weekly training volume of 50–60km maintained for at least 3–4 months prior to the attempt.
  • Sustained rough water swimming — you must be able to maintain efficient stroke mechanics in 1.5–2m swell for 15+ hours.
  • Night swimming experience: most attempts start between midnight and 3am and run through the following day.
  • Jellyfish sting response: know your personal reaction to jellyfish stings and have a treatment protocol agreed with your crew and medical advisor.

Unlike most Oceans Seven crossings, the Molokai Channel does not require cold water acclimatisation — at 24–28°C the water is comfortably warm throughout the season. The preparation focus instead shifts entirely to ultra-distance endurance in rough ocean conditions. Swimmers who train primarily in pools or protected harbours often find the relentless Pacific swell far more energy-consuming than their training suggested. Build in as much open ocean swell swimming as possible in the months before your attempt, even if this means travelling to exposed coastlines for training camps.

Nutrition over 15–20 hours is a significant logistical challenge. Most Ka'iwi Channel swimmers consume 600–800 calories per hour in liquid form, primarily carbohydrate-based drinks with sodium supplementation. Crew briefing is critical — they need to maintain your feeding schedule precisely, monitor your stroke count and pace, and manage the boat safely in open Pacific conditions. Engage an experienced Hawaiian crew well in advance; local knowledge of the channel's currents is an enormous advantage for your pilot in choosing the correct start angle and adjusting for drift during the swim.

Frequently Asked Questions
How do I plan around the box jellyfish cycle?
Box jellyfish in Hawaii bloom approximately 9–10 nights after each full moon, with the peak typically on night 9–10 and tapering off over 2–3 nights. Identify the full moon dates during your available window, count forward 9–10 days, and avoid those nights. Your target swim window should be at least 4–5 days before or after a predicted bloom. Local marine biologists and experienced Hawaii channel crews can advise on current seasonal patterns.
Is the Molokai Channel governed by a specific organisation?
Unlike some Oceans Seven crossings, the Ka'iwi Channel does not have a single dedicated governing body equivalent to the CCSF or CSSA. Ratification for Oceans Seven purposes is handled through the Oceans Seven governing framework. Swimmers organise their own sanctioned attempts with locally approved observers and pilots. The Hawaii Swimming Association and experienced local operators can advise on the requirements for a ratified crossing.
What is the shark situation — should I be worried?
Tiger sharks are a genuine risk that all Ka'iwi Channel swimmers accept. The channel's deep water and proximity to reef systems that support prey species make it active shark habitat. Your escort boat provides the primary deterrent — sharks typically avoid large vessels. Some swimmers use electronic shark deterrent devices. There have been no fatal attacks during sanctioned Ka'iwi Channel attempts, but sharks are seen regularly by support crews.
Can I swim it in winter?
Winter swims (October–March) are possible but much more challenging. North Pacific storms generate large northwest swells that stack on top of the resident trade-wind swell, producing chaotic 3–5m seas. The few winter crossings on record are exceptional feats of rough-water swimming. The April–September window offers significantly more manageable conditions for a first crossing.
Where do I finish on Oahu?
The traditional finish is Sandy Beach on Oahu's southeast coast (also known as Makapuu area). Depending on currents during the swim, some crossings finish slightly north or south of Sandy Beach — your pilot will adjust the target finish based on actual drift experienced during the crossing. A shore touch is required for ratification.
