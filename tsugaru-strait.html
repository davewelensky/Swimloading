Japan — Oceans Seven

TSUGARU STRAIT

The 19.5km crossing between Japan's Honshu and Hokkaido islands is the most logistically complex of the Oceans Seven — an intensely bureaucratic process, a brief annual window, violent currents, and cold upwelling that can drop water temperature by 8°C mid-crossing without warning.

19.5 km 15–20°C Very Hard Aug – Sep
Key Facts
Distance
19.5 km
Tappi Saki, Honshu to Shirakami Misaki, Hokkaido. Strong currents mean the actual distance swum is often significantly longer as swimmers are pushed off the straight-line route.
Water Temperature
15–20°C
Surface water in August–September reaches 18–20°C, but cold upwelling from the Tsugaru Current can plunge temperatures to 10–12°C at any moment. The real cold risk is unpredictable, not constant.
Swim Season
Aug – Sep
The viable window is extremely narrow — effectively August and early September. Outside this, typhoon season, cold water, and severe currents make the crossing practically impossible.
Average Finish Time
7–12 hrs
Record: 7h 6m. Many swimmers take 10–13 hours due to current drift adding significant distance. Some attempts never reach Hokkaido despite hours of effort against adverse currents.
Governing Body
TCSA
Tsugaru Channel Swimming Association (津軽海峡水泳協会). Japanese-language coordination required. TCSA manages all sanctions, observer assignments, and liaison with Japan Coast Guard.
Difficulty
Very Hard
One of the Oceans Seven. The primary difficulty is not the distance but the current variability, cold upwelling, logistical complexity, and the extraordinarily brief annual weather window.
What Makes It Challenging

Primary Hazards

  • Cold upwelling: the Tsugaru Current carries warm Kuroshio water northeast, but deep cold water upwells without warning — surface temperature can drop from 20°C to 10°C within metres, causing sudden cold shock in a swimmer who felt comfortable moments before.
  • Current variability: the strait lies between two major current systems (Tsugaru Current and Liman Cold Current) whose interaction creates unpredictable, shifting current patterns that no tidal table can fully capture.
  • Extremely narrow weather window: typhoons regularly affect Japan's northern waters from July through September, and the Tsugaru Strait is frequently closed to small vessels by Japan Coast Guard during typhoon watches. A week's window may realistically offer only 1–2 viable swim days.
  • Logistical complexity: all correspondence with TCSA and authorities is in Japanese; obtaining vessel permits, Coast Guard notifications, and observer assignments requires extensive advance planning, often with a Japanese-speaking intermediary.

Conditions Overview

  • The strait sees strong winds from both north and south depending on synoptic patterns — calm days with manageable swell are relatively rare in the August–September window.
  • Fog is extremely common in the Tsugaru Strait, particularly in early morning — the Japan Coast Guard may ground escort boats in zero-visibility fog, aborting a swim already underway.
  • Ferry traffic between Honshu and Hokkaido (particularly the Tsugaru Kaikyo Ferry route) creates hazardous vessel wash and requires active management by the pilot vessel.
  • The strait marks the boundary between the Sea of Japan and the Pacific Ocean, where two distinct water masses meet — the oceanographic complexity this creates drives the unpredictable conditions swimmers face.
About The Crossing

The Tsugaru Strait (津軽海峡, Tsugaru Kaikyo) separates the Japanese islands of Honshu and Hokkaido at Japan's northeastern extremity. At its narrowest point the strait is approximately 19.5km wide, connecting the Sea of Japan to the west with the Pacific Ocean (specifically the Tsugaru Strait section of the western North Pacific) to the east. The crossing runs from the cape of Tappi Saki on the Aomori Prefecture coast of Honshu to Shirakami Misaki on the southern tip of Hokkaido.

The Tsugaru Strait is one of the most important strategic waterways in East Asia — it is one of the few deep-water passages through which large vessels (including submarines) can transit between the Pacific and the Sea of Japan. This military and commercial significance means the Japan Coast Guard maintains active oversight of all vessel movements in the strait, adding a layer of regulatory complexity to any crossing attempt that does not exist for most other marathon swims in the world.

The Tsugaru Channel Swimming Association was established to support and ratify crossings of the strait and to manage the necessary government coordination. Because the TCSA operates entirely in Japanese and most of its processes are documented only in Japanese, international swimmers face a significant communication barrier. Unlike other Oceans Seven crossings where English-language information and local English-speaking crews are readily available, Tsugaru crossings typically require either fluency in Japanese or a trusted Japanese-speaking intermediary to coordinate the logistics. This practical difficulty is itself part of what makes the crossing rare and prestigious.

The first recorded crossing is attributed to a Japanese swimmer in 1955. The crossing remains one of the least-attempted of the Oceans Seven, with only a handful of successful international completions each year — far fewer than Gibraltar or the Catalina Channel. The combination of logistical difficulty, narrow weather window, and unpredictable cold upwelling means that many Oceans Seven chasers who successfully complete the other six spend years waiting for a Tsugaru attempt to align.

Preparation Guide

Training Benchmarks

  • Sustained swimming in cold water, including experience with sudden temperature drops — acclimatise to entering 10–12°C water after extended swimming in 18–20°C water.
  • Longest single open water swim of at least 15km in exposed ocean conditions.
  • Weekly volume of 35–45km maintained through the spring and summer before your attempt.
  • Typhoon contingency planning: physical readiness to attempt on any viable day within your window means being match-fit for the entire 2–3 week stay in Japan.
  • Begin TCSA contact and logistics at least 12–18 months before your target season — the paperwork and permits required are substantial.

The cold upwelling hazard unique to the Tsugaru Strait deserves specific preparation attention. Unlike Cook Strait where the water is consistently cold throughout, Tsugaru can feel like warm water swimming for hours before suddenly delivering a pocket of 10°C deep water. The physiological shock of this transition — when your body has been warm and relaxed — can be more dangerous than sustained cold water swimming. Train specifically for this by practising deliberate transitions from warm to cold water during long sessions, building your nervous system's ability to adapt quickly without panicking or losing stroke efficiency.

Logistics preparation should begin 12–18 months before your target crossing season. Contact the TCSA (ideally through a Japanese-speaking channel) to understand the current requirements for sanctioned attempts. Engage an experienced Japanese escort boat captain — the pool of operators willing and permitted to escort marathon swimmers in this heavily regulated strait is small. Many international swimmers work through a Japan-based open water swimming contact or sports tourism operator who can navigate the bureaucratic requirements. Budget substantially more time and money than for any other Oceans Seven crossing for the logistics phase alone.

Frequently Asked Questions
Why is the Tsugaru Strait considered so logistically difficult?
Three factors combine to make Tsugaru the most logistically challenging Oceans Seven crossing. First, the Japan Coast Guard oversight of the strait means formal vessel permits and notifications are required — not just courtesy calls. Second, the TCSA operates entirely in Japanese with limited English-language support for international swimmers. Third, the narrow weather window (effectively a few weeks in August–September) concentrates demand and leaves almost no flexibility if paperwork isn't completed months in advance. The swim itself is not the hardest part of a Tsugaru attempt — getting to the start line is.
What is the cold upwelling and how do I prepare for it?
The Tsugaru Strait sits at the boundary between the warm Tsugaru Current (carrying Kuroshio water northeast) and cold deep Pacific water. The interface between these water masses is not stable — cold, dense water periodically rises to the surface (upwells) in localised patches. A swimmer can be at 19°C surface water and suddenly enter a patch of 10–11°C upwelled deep water. The transition is abrupt and the patch may be 50–200m wide. Prepare by deliberately practising cold water entry after warm swimming, and mentally rehearsing your response to sudden cold shock: maintain stroke, breathe steadily, do not panic.
How do I contact the Tsugaru Channel Swimming Association?
The TCSA is based in Aomori Prefecture and primarily communicates in Japanese. The most reliable approach for international swimmers is to connect via the global Oceans Seven community — other swimmers who have completed Tsugaru can often provide current contact details and introductions. Some Japanese sports tourism operators specialise in facilitating international marathon swim attempts and can manage TCSA liaison on your behalf. Allow at least 12–18 months of lead time for the full sanctioning and permit process.
What direction is the swim?
The standard ratified direction is Honshu (Tappi Saki, Aomori Prefecture) to Hokkaido (Shirakami Misaki). This direction is preferred because the Tsugaru Current generally flows northeast through the strait, providing a mild assist and a more predictable drift pattern. The reverse crossing is technically possible but significantly harder and rarely attempted. Both directions are accepted for Oceans Seven ratification purposes.
How does Tsugaru compare to the other Oceans Seven crossings?
In terms of raw distance (19.5km), Tsugaru is one of the shorter Oceans Seven crossings — comparable to the Rottnest Channel Swim in straight-line distance. But it is consistently ranked among the most difficult Oceans Seven crossings due to the combination of cold upwelling, current variability, narrow weather window, logistical complexity, and the psychological challenge of an attempt that may be weather-aborted after years of preparation. Many Oceans Seven completists describe Tsugaru as the crossing that took the most attempts and the most total elapsed time between first application and successful completion.
