CATALINA CHANNEL

One of the original marathon swims and a member of the Triple Crown of Open Water Swimming. The 33km crossing from Catalina Island's Two Harbors to the California mainland demands cold water tolerance, shark awareness, and relentless mental fortitude.

What Makes It Challenging

Primary Hazards

  • Blue sharks (Prionace glauca) and occasional shortfin mako sharks are common in the Santa Catalina Basin — shark watches are mandatory.
  • Portuguese man-o-war and moon jellies can appear in concentrated blooms, causing stings across face and shoulders.
  • Cold thermocline upwelling mid-channel can drop water temp by 4–6°C within metres, triggering cold shock.
  • Heavy commercial and recreational vessel traffic in the San Pedro Channel requires escort boats to maintain safety perimeter.

Conditions Overview

  • Prevailing northwest swells (1–2m) create an uncomfortable side chop for most of the crossing.
  • Fog is common in June and July, reducing visibility to near zero — GPS navigation and horn signals become critical.
  • Wind picks up in the afternoon, so most swimmers aim to finish before 2pm; a midnight start is standard.
  • Kelp forests near both coastlines can entangle swimmers and must be navigated carefully at the start and finish.
About The Crossing

The Catalina Channel separates Santa Catalina Island from the California mainland across the San Pedro Channel. The first recorded crossing was completed by Canadian swimmer George Young in 1927, who won the Wrigley Ocean Marathon, a race organised by chewing gum magnate William Wrigley Jr. with a $25,000 prize. Young completed the swim in 15 hours 44 minutes, beating 102 other contestants in what was one of the most watched sporting events of the era.

The standard sanctioned route runs from the Isthmus Cove area at Two Harbors on Catalina's north coast to the mainland finish at Point Vicente Lighthouse on the Palos Verdes Peninsula. Swimmers may also complete the reverse (mainland to island) or "double Catalina" crossing — both count toward the Triple Crown. Diana Nyad completed the crossing in 1977 and holds a notable place in the channel's history.

The Catalina Channel Swimming Federation, founded in 1981, sets the rules for sanctioned swims. English Channel rules apply: no wetsuit, no touching the boat, and solo or relay attempts must be ratified by an official observer. Relay teams of up to 6 swimmers are common, with each swimmer taking 1-hour rotations.

The crossing is part of the Triple Crown of Open Water Swimming alongside the English Channel and the Manhattan Island Marathon Swim — a recognition given to swimmers who complete all three. As of 2024, fewer than 250 individuals have achieved the Triple Crown.

Preparation Guide

Training Benchmarks

  • Longest single open water swim of at least 20–25km before your attempt.
  • Weekly training volume of 40–50km in the 3 months prior to the crossing.
  • Sustained swimming in water below 16°C — cold acclimation is non-negotiable.
  • Night swimming experience: most crossings start in darkness and last until late morning.
  • Feed practice from a moving boat — you will need to eat and drink every 30–45 minutes without stopping.

Most coaches recommend a 12–18 month preparation window for first-time Catalina Channel aspirants. The key physical demands are different from pool marathon swimming: you need cold acclimatisation more than raw speed, and the ability to maintain your pace when jellies sting and the thermocline kicks in at hour six. Build a base of long open water swims through winter to train your body to stay efficient in 14–16°C water.

Nutrition is a critical discipline. Over a 12–14 hour swim you will consume 4,000–6,000 calories, primarily from liquid feeds (maltodextrin-based drinks, warm broth, gels). Practice your feed protocol extensively in training so it becomes automatic. Your escort crew — captain, observer, and at least two support people — need to be briefed on the CCSF rules and your feeding schedule. Engage a local Catalina crew well in advance; experienced captains book out a year ahead.

Frequently Asked Questions
Do I need to swim from island to mainland, or can I go the other direction?
Both directions are accepted by CCSF for ratification. Most swimmers go island to mainland (Two Harbors to Pt Vicente) as prevailing currents are slightly more favourable, but the reverse route is equally valid for the Triple Crown.
Are wetsuits allowed?
No. CCSF rules mirror English Channel rules — only a standard swimsuit, cap, and goggles are permitted for a ratified solo crossing. Relay swimmers rotate under the same rules. Wetsuits disqualify a ratified attempt.
How do I apply for a sanctioned crossing?
Apply through the CCSF website (swimcatalina.org). You submit a swim history demonstrating readiness, pay the sanctioning fee, and arrange your own escort boat from the CCSF-approved captain list. Observer assignment comes from CCSF. Slots fill months in advance.
What happens if there are sharks?
Blue sharks are common and generally not aggressive toward swimmers. Your escort boat crew maintains a watch and will advise you if they feel a shark poses a threat. The swimmer makes the final decision whether to continue. There are no recorded shark attacks on Catalina Channel swimmers, but the risk is real and must be accepted before you start.
What is the typical cost of a Catalina Channel crossing?
Budget USD $3,000–$5,000 for the attempt including escort boat charter (the largest expense at $2,000–$3,500), CCSF sanctioning fees, observer fees, travel, and accommodation on Catalina the night before. Relay teams split the boat cost between swimmers.
