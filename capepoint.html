Cape Point is one of the most dramatic swims on the planet. You start on the cold Atlantic side and finish in the warmer, calmer waters of False Bay — swimming around the tip of the Cape Peninsula.
Route Description
01
Start — Diaz Beach
Enter at Diaz Beach on the Atlantic/southwestern face of Cape Point within the Cape of Good Hope Nature Reserve. Cold water entry — Benguela upwelling keeps temps at 13–16°C. The beach is exposed to SW swell: this is your go/no-go moment. If swell is above 1.5m at entry, abort.
02
The Cape — rounding the headland
Swim south and then east around the Cape Point headland. This is the most exposed section — expect strong currents as the Benguela and Agulhas systems meet near the point. The current pattern varies with tide: on an ebb (outgoing) tide the flow runs south-to-east around the point, assisting the swimmer. On flood, it can work against you. Swell wraps around the cape — stay focused. Support vessel essential here.
03
Entering False Bay
Once around the headland, conditions change dramatically. Swell drops as you enter the shelter of False Bay. Water temperature rises 2–3°C over the last few kilometres. The transition from cold choppy Atlantic to calmer, warmer False Bay water is one of the most striking experiences in open water swimming.
04
Finish — Millers Point
Exit at Millers Point on the False Bay side, approximately 3km south of Simon's Town. Clear water exit unassisted. The finishing section is sheltered and typically calm, even when the Atlantic start was rough.
Key Requirements
01
Swell below 1.5m at Diaz Beach — go/no-go threshold
This is the critical gate for the Cape Point swim. Diaz Beach faces directly into the SW Atlantic swell fetch. Swell above 1.5m makes entry dangerous and the rounding extremely demanding. Above 2m is a no-go. Check the Forecast tab for the 7-day swell outlook.
02
No sustained SE wind
SE wind (the Cape Doctor) creates difficult conditions around the point and pushes against the swimmer from Millers Point side. SE above 15 km/h is a caution; above 25 km/h is a no-go. NW or N wind is ideal — it shelters the headland approach and assists the False Bay section.
03
Cape of Good Hope Nature Reserve permit
The swim takes place within the Cape of Good Hope section of Table Mountain National Park. A permit and prior approval from SANParks is required. Swimmer and support vessel must be cleared in advance. The reserve gate opens at specific times — coordinate your start accordingly.
04
Cold water — minimum 13°C viable for skins
Atlantic entry at Diaz Beach is typically 13–16°C year-round due to Benguela upwelling. Cold shock on entry is a real risk. Acclimatise beforehand, enter slowly. The warm False Bay finish (16–18°C) provides some relief for the final kilometres. Minimum 13°C for skins; below that, serious hypothermia risk for a 2.5–4 hour swim.
05
Experienced motorised support vessel — non-negotiable
The support skipper must know the Cape Point headland currents and have experience in exposed Atlantic conditions. Navigation around the cape with breaking waves and current requires a confident skipper. The swim cannot proceed without proper vessel support. A secondary kayak escort is strongly recommended for the headland section.
The Cape Point Current — What to Expect
Cape Point sits where two of the world's great ocean systems interact — the cold Benguela (northward along the west coast) and the warm Agulhas (southwestward along the east coast). This creates complex, dynamic current patterns around the headland that vary by tide, season, and weather.

On a good day: The southward Benguela surface current assists the swimmer from Diaz Beach toward the point. Once around, residual Agulhas eddy circulation in False Bay can provide mild eastward assist toward Millers Point.

On a bad day: Strong tidal currents around the headland can push the swimmer off course or back. Current speeds of 1–2 knots are possible in the channel near the point.

Navigation note: Your escort skipper must constantly read and communicate current direction. Do not fight the current — work with it by adjusting your angle of approach to the headland.
Wildlife — What Shares the Water
The Cape of Good Hope is one of the most biodiverse marine areas on earth. Expect:

· Cape fur seals — common around the cape, curious and harmless
· African penguins — occasionally seen along the route
· Great white sharks — present in these waters. Risk is real and must be factored into safety planning. Shark spotters do not operate at this location. Discuss with your support team and consider appropriate shark deterrent equipment.
· Southern right whales — seasonal (May–November), occasionally approach swimmers
· Sunfish (Mola mola) — sometimes seen in the area, harmless

What Affects Cape Point Time
Swell penalty:
· <1.0m — no penalty, clean swimming
· 1.0–1.5m — −0.2 km/h (15–20 min slower)
· 1.5–2.0m — −0.4 km/h (30–40 min slower)
· 2.0m+ — not recommended

Temperature gradient:
Atlantic start (Diaz Beach) is typically 13–15°C. False Bay finish (Millers Point) is 16–18°C. Cold water slows pace in the first kilometre — factor in 5–10 min extra for acclimatisation to cold entry.

Current:
Highly variable. Favourable current around the headland can save 20–30 min. Adverse current can add the same. This is the largest uncertainty in time prediction.
